4 sentences of Aramaic in *Shabbos* 5b.

12 steps underneath them, and not one of the 12 is written in the Gemara.

Ramchal counts them out in chapter 10: two claims, four syllogisms, one
elucidation, reconstructed line by line until every premise lands on something
nobody argues with. Then he says the thing that has bothered me for years. Leave
one step out and you have not understood the argument.

He is right, and look at what being right costs. Four sentences took him pages.
The longest passage I labelled for this site runs 77 sentences, and that is two
dapim of *Bava Kamma*.

## The sefer I own and did not learn

I bought *Derech Tevunos* because Ramchal wrote it. I learned *Mesillas
Yesharim*, like everybody. I learned *Derech Hashem*. This one I opened twice,
filed it as the logic one, and put it back on the shelf, where it has been
keeping company with a lot of other people's copies.

## What is in it

*Derech Tevunos* is a specification.

Chapter 9 takes every sentence a sugya can contain and gives it a name: 7
elements, 19 leaves, each one a thing a sentence does to a sentence before it,
each with a test that separates it from the label sitting next to it. Chapter 3
says that whatever the surface was, a question, a flourish, an ellipsis you
finish in your head, the claim underneath is a subject and a predicate in one of
11 manners, and you put it in that form before you do anything else with it.
Chapter 4 says how two statements can stand to each other, including the case
where two Tannaim look like they are arguing and are not. Chapter 11 gives 24
<bdi lang="he">הבחנות</bdi>, the distinctions a subject can be examined along.

Chapters 7 and 8 say what each move does to the claim it hits, and that is the
part I would put in front of anybody.

A <bdi lang="he">סתירה</bdi> shows a claim is false, and the claim is rejected.
A <bdi lang="he">דחיה</bdi> shows only that the proof could be read another way,
and the claim drops back to doubt. A <bdi lang="he">פירכא</bdi> never rejects
anything at all; it unsettles, and unsettling is the whole of what it is
permitted to do. Doubt, for Ramchal, is where every claim starts and where it
returns the moment its support is taken away.

Knocking down a proof does not prove the negation. He says it flat, in chapter
8, and almost nobody's instincts run that way. A sugya is full of moves that feel
like victories and are not, and he is the one who tells you which are which.

Or take his handling of <bdi lang="he">אם</bdi>. A conditional asserts the
dependency and nothing else, so a statement whose halves are both true but
unrelated is false, and one whose halves are both false is true when the
dependency is real. Material implication calls the first of those true. Ramchal
calls it false, in 1742, and he is the one describing how a sugya argues.

## Why nobody runs it

The procedure is sound, and the procedure is expensive.

*Bava Metzia* 21b to 22b runs 57 sentences. Abaye states a claim in the first
one, it comes under attack 10 separate times over the next 33 rows, and the
support that finally lands on it sits in row 51. Labelling that by hand, every
label naming the row it acts on, is an afternoon. Then you have one page.

There is a second problem sitting under the first one. A talmid chacham already
does most of this in his head, fast, with no names for any of it. The person
equipped to produce the labels has no use for them, and the person who would
gain from them cannot pay for them. A method whose only qualified users have
already outgrown it does not go anywhere.

## What changed

The expensive half got cheap, and I want to be precise about which half.

The question a model answers here is narrow. What does this sentence do to the
sentence before it? The answer has to be one of 19 labels, it has to name the
sentence it acts on, and it has to survive Ramchal's own test for telling that
label from its nearest neighbour. An answer outside the vocabulary fails
validation and the file never draws. That is clerical work against a closed
list, which is the one kind of work these things are reliable at.

So I ran it. There are 9 passages on this site, 5 of Ramchal's own examples and
4 opening sugyot. For each one a model proposed the labelling and I checked it
by hand, sentence by sentence. It got things wrong. I have no measured error rate
for you and I am not going to invent one.

What made the checking survivable is the drawing. A sentence labelled wrong
takes the picture with it: a resolution floating with nothing above it to
resolve, a rail running to the wrong row, a fold closing over an argument that
never settled. You catch it before you have finished reading the row.

## The drawing is doing more work than the labels

Here's the thing I would rather not say out loud.

Very few people are interested in the deep analysis of dialectical process. A
great many people are interested in a well-made drawing with a good icon set. I
am on the third generation of the icon set, and that is where the reach is.

If this sefer gets used, it will be because somebody saw the shape of an
argument, found it beautiful, and got curious about what produced it.

## What I am not claiming

The machine sorts sentences into categories a person specified. It does not
learn, it has no yiras shamayim, and it replaces no rebbi, no chavrusa and no
beis medrash. Anybody promising otherwise is selling something.

Ramchal opens the sefer by asking that we look at the claims and not at the ones
making them, and then spends 11 chapters teaching a person how to do that. He
wrote a manual. Manuals are for using.

## The ask

The passages are [drawn](/sugya). The sefer is [here](/docs), Hebrew and
English, all 11 chapters, and [chapter 9](/docs/text/chapter-09) is the
vocabulary the drawing reads. A file you label yourself gets
[drawn by the same code](/byo) these were.

If you find a sentence I labelled wrong, I want to hear about it. Every label on
this site is a claim about a sugya, and a claim about a sugya is something I am
allowed to be wrong about.
